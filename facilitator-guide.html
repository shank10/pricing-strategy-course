INSTRUCTOR GUIDE: Strategic Product Pricing (MBA Seminar)

Duration: 2.5 Hours · Objective: Equip MBA candidates with the frameworks to determine value capture across diverse business models, shifting from basic cost-plus math to strategic, behavioral, and dynamic pricing models.

🕒 First 30 mins: The Foundations of Pricing Strategy

Goal: Establish the foundational vocabulary. Move the cohort away from “cost” and toward “value.”

1. The Pricing Triad (Framework)

  • Cost-Based (The Floor): Necessary for survival but terrible for strategy. It ignores the buyer’s perceived value.
  • Competition-Based (The Middle): Anchoring against rivals.
  • Value-Based (The Ceiling): Pricing based on customer Willingness to Pay (WTP). This is where consulting firms focus.
Instructor Prompt: “What happens if our competitor priced their product wrong to begin with? We just copy their mistake.” “What did Jio do to bankrupt five other telecom service providers?”

2. Market Entry Strategies

  • Skimming (High to Low): Capturing the consumer surplus of early adopters.
    • Global Case: Apple iPhone — Launch at top dollar, capture die-hards, drop prices as demand softens.
    • Indian Case: Ather Energy — Entered the Indian 2-wheeler market at a steep premium (~₹1.5L+), targeting tech-savvy urban early adopters before scaling down into mass-market variants like the 450S.
  • Penetration (Low to High): Buying market share to build a moat or network effect.
    • Global Case: Netflix / Disney+ — Unsustainably low entry pricing to kill linear TV, followed by systematic price hikes once the habit is formed.
    • Indian Case Study: The Jio Playbook — Reliance Jio (2016) executed one of the most aggressive penetration pricing strategies in global corporate history. By offering 7 months of completely free high-speed 4G data and voice calls, Jio built a critical mass of 100M+ users in record time. Once incumbents were financially strained, Jio monetized through steady ARPU tariff hikes and ecosystem lock-in.

3. Behavioral & Psychological Tactics

Transition Script: “We like to think buyers are rational calculators of value. They aren’t. Context dictates perception.”

The Decoy Effect

  • Global Case: The Economist — Web ($59) | Print ($125) | Web+Print ($125). The “Print only” option serves as a dummy to make Web+Print look irresistible.
  • Indian Case: Times Prime / Zomato Gold — Structuring multi-tier subscription plans where a middle plan (e.g., 3-month pass at ₹299) is deliberately priced so close to the annual plan (₹499) that the customer feels foolish choosing anything other than the annual subscription.

Price Anchoring

  • Present a high initial price as the anchor so subsequent lower-priced options feel like exceptional bargains, steering buyer perception of value.

The Framing Effect

  • Frame the same cost differently, such as “₹30 per day” instead of “₹900 per month,” or emphasize percentage savings rather than the absolute discount amount.

Charm Pricing (Left-Digit Effect)

  • Use .99, .95, or .98 endings to exploit the left-digit bias, making ₹199.99 feel substantially cheaper than ₹200 even though the actual difference is minimal.

Partitioned Pricing

  • Separate the base product price from mandatory extras like shipping or handling, relying on consumers’ tendency to underweight later-added costs.

Mental Accounting & Partitioned Bundling

  • Package multiple items or features into one all-inclusive price to reduce the pain of paying separately for each component.

Prestige / Veblen Pricing

  • Keep prices intentionally high to signal luxury, status, or superior quality—making the product desirable precisely because it is expensive.

Reference Price Manipulation

  • Show a struck-through original price beside the sale price to create a mental benchmark of high savings and influence perceived value.
Interactive 5-Minute Activity “Name That Strategy” — put three real-world pricing moves on the screen. Give students 30 seconds to identify which strategy from the board they are using, and why.

🕒 Spend 90 to 100 mins covering all the 5 modules: Module 1 & 2 - The Physical World

Goal: Contrast pricing power in high-CapEx heavy industry versus fast-moving consumer goods.

Module 1: Complex Physical Products (B2B & High-Ticket B2C)

  • Core Challenge: Long sales cycles, high R&D, and massive capital expenditure. The initial purchase price is often just the tip of the iceberg.
  • Global Case Study: Siemens / Alstom (Rolling Stock)
    • The Nuance: Selling “rolling stock availability” rather than just train cars.
    • Value-in-Use Pricing: Buyers care about Total Cost of Ownership (TCO). Manufacturers cut train purchase margins in exchange for lucrative 30-year Long-Term Service Agreements for parts, maintenance, and software updates.
  • Indian Case Study: Tata Motors (Nexon EV & EV Fleet Strategy)
    • The Context: How do you price an electric car in a deeply price-sensitive market with zero EV infrastructure?
    • The Strategy: Tata Motors priced the Nexon EV at a sweet spot (~15–20% premium over its ICE equivalent). They calculated the TCO payback period to match the Indian buyer’s psychological threshold: a driver doing 30 km/day breaks even on the price premium within ~2.5 years via lower running costs. This value-in-use pricing turned Tata Motors into India’s dominant EV manufacturer with >70% market share.

Module 2: Fast-Moving Consumer Goods (FMCG)

  • Core Challenge: Intense shelf competition, low switching costs, highly elastic demand.
  • Global Case Study: P&G (Tide)
    • Price Pack Architecture (PPA): P&G sells massive 150-load tubs at Costco (low margin, high volume) alongside small single-use packs for convenience stores (high margin per ounce).
  • Indian Case Study: CavinKare & HUL (The Sachet Revolution & Low Unit Pricing)
    • The Context: In rural India, low daily-wage earners cannot afford a ₹150 bottle of shampoo, regardless of the cost-per-ml value.
    • The Innovation: CavinKare pioneered the ₹1 Chik Shampoo sachet, unlocking an enormous market of low-income rural consumers.
    • The Strategic Response: Hindustan Unilever rebuilt its Price Pack Architecture around Magic Price Points (₹1, ₹2, ₹5, ₹10). Instead of changing prices when raw material costs fluctuate, they hold the price point rigid and adjust the volume via grammage reduction, making shrinkflation appear seamless.

🕒 Module 3 & 4 - Knowledge and Software

Goal: Explore models where the Marginal Cost of Production drops toward zero.

Module 3: Professional Services (Consulting, IT Services)

  • Core Challenge: Selling invisible expertise constrained by human hours.
  • Global Case Study: Tier 1 Strategy Firms
    • The Nuance: Selling risk mitigation to CEOs. The premium price ($1M+ for a 6-week study) is a signal of quality and an executive insurance policy (“Nobody gets fired for hiring McKinsey”).
  • Indian Case Study: TCS, Infosys, & Wipro (The IT Services Transition)
    • The Traditional Model: Indian IT majors built multi-billion-dollar businesses on Time & Materials billing per developer hour ($25–$50/hr offshore).
    • The Disruption & Pivot: As GenAI and hyper-automation cut the required human hours for code generation and testing by 40–50%, traditional T&M models threaten revenue. Indian IT majors are shifting toward Outcome-Based and Fixed-Price Contracts—charging clients based on uptime, deployment speed, or business deliverables rather than developer headcount.

Module 4: B2B SaaS (Software as a Service)

  • Core Challenge: High fixed upfront engineering costs, zero marginal replication cost.
  • Global Case Study: Salesforce
    • Land and Expand: Sell a core CRM module cheaply to one team, build high switching costs, and upsell complementary modules (Marketing Cloud, Service Cloud).
  • Indian Case Study: The “Girias of SaaS” — Zoho & Freshworks
    • The Context: Building enterprise software in India for global SMBs and mid-market enterprises.
    • The Strategy: Value-Disruption Pricing — Zoho and Freshworks undercut incumbents by offering ~80% of the core enterprise features at ~20–30% of the price point. By leveraging lower R&D overhead in India, they achieve high operating margins while serving price-sensitive SMB segments that Salesforce ignores.
    • Unbundling: Zoho introduced transparent, modular per-user pricing with no long-term multi-year lock-in contracts, lowering friction for adoption in emerging markets.

🕒 Module 5 & Wrap-Up - The Algorithmic Frontier

Goal: Understand real-time yield management and tie all frameworks together.

Module 5: Dynamic Pricing & Two-Sided Marketplaces

  • Core Challenge: Balancing volatile supply and demand in real-time while extracting maximum consumer surplus.
  • Global Case Study: Uber / DoorDash
    • Surge Pricing: Uses algorithmic pricing to ration excess demand and incentivize supply (drivers) onto the network during peak hours.
  • Indian Case Study: Swiggy, Zomato, & Quick-Commerce
    • Hyper-Local Elasticity: Food delivery and quick-commerce platforms in India operate in one of the world’s most price-sensitive consumer markets.
    • Platform Fee Creep: To achieve profitability without driving away orders, Zomato and Swiggy introduced an unbundled Platform Fee (starting at ₹2 per order, incrementally ratcheted up to ₹6–₹10+). Consumers treat small line items as negligible friction, allowing platforms to generate high-margin revenue on top of standard restaurant commissions.
    • Dynamic Handling & Rain Surge: During monsoons or late nights, algorithms dynamically apply “Rain Fees” or “Surge Handling” based on hyper-local rider availability mapped down to micro-geographies.

🎯 Wrap-Up & Final Framework

Conclude by tying all five models back to a single unifying concept: Defensibility.

“Price is simply the exchange rate on the value you’ve created. Whether you are CavinKare selling a ₹1 shampoo sachet in rural Tamil Nadu or Salesforce selling $150/seat enterprise CRM software in San Francisco, if you create a commodity, your price will be driven down to the cost of production. If you create a monopoly—through physical scale, brand equity, intellectual property, or network effects—you earn the right to price based on value.”
